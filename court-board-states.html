Control desk — court board states

Every state the Desk's court board can display, and every situation each one can render in. A court state is derived, never stored: the router keeps a closed set of match statuses, while a court's state is computed from the matches on it and the clock. This page is a static mirror of the real tile — same tokens, same strings, same predicates — read out of src/lib/tournament/court-state.ts, desk.ts and the tile's own JSX in desk.tsx. Hover an enabled tile: the lift is the app's own.

Read the tiles as a wall, not one at a time. The pill is the label, and the tint is the second channel: the tile is a wash of the state and the pill is the denser surface, in every state without exception. Tiles are <button>s, so a court with no target is dimmed and inert rather than hidden — it is still readable on the wall.

2. How a state is derived

deriveCourtState() takes one court and the day's matches and applies these tests in order. First match wins, so a court is in exactly one state.

OrderTestStatePill
1A match on this court is in-progress, and elapsed minutes are within the slot for its kindplayingPLAYING
2A match on this court is in-progress, and elapsed minutes exceed the slot for its kindoverrunOVERRUN
3No match in progress; the court's earliest scheduled match with a time has readiness readyfree-readyREADY
4No match in progress; that match's readiness is resting, player-busy or teams-missingfree-blockedWAITING
5No match in progress and no scheduled match with a time on this courtidleIDLE

The slot comes from the tournament's own timings — pool and placement matches are measured with group_slot_minutes, draws and brackets with knockout_slot_minutes (defaults 20 and 25; rest gaps 10 and 30). That mapping lives in one place (deskKindPhase), so the board, the overrun alert and the start check cannot disagree.

State 1 of 5 — green

3. playing — a match is live and inside its slot

The only green state, and the only one whose elapsed time is measured. Everything below is reachable: 1–5 differ by the queue behind the court, 6 by who is signed in, 7 by a missing start instant. A court with a match on it names its next pairing and nothing more — no readiness line, because the question it answers cannot be acted on yet.

4. overrun — a match is live and past its slot

A delay, not a fault, so it is amber rather than red. Unlike playing it turns the elapsed line bold warn ink; the line itself stays a single fact — how long the match has been on — because the tile already says it is running long, and a +N over slot or started N min late clause beside it was arithmetic competing with the number a moderator actually reads. It raises the "N min on court" row in Needs action once it is a third of its slot past it (five minutes at least), or sooner if the match behind it is already late — see 9.2 and 9.3.

5. free-ready — the court is free and its next match can start

This state requires a queued match, so it never falls back to the "ready to place" line. It is also the only state that raises the dispatch prompt — the one action the desk wants taken.

6. free-blocked — the court is free but its next match is not ready

Also requires a queued match. Asks nothing of the moderator: the line under the tile names the specific reason instead. The chip is a deeper grey than idle's, which is the only thing separating the two fills.

7. idle — nothing on court and nothing scheduled

No playing match and no scheduled match with a time. It can still be actionable: if a ready match holds no court, an idle tile is an enabled button offering that pairing. That tile is also the only place an unplaced match appears — Needs action deliberately has no row for it, because the decision is the organiser's, not the desk's (see 9.2).

8. The pieces every tile is assembled from

8.1 The four readiness lines

Drawn only on a free court — free-ready or free-blocked — that has a queued match with a time. A court with a match on it (playing, overrun) names its next pairing and stops there: the readiness line answers "can the queued match start now", and a court that is still occupied cannot answer yes whatever the clock says. Evaluated in this order, first match wins; the last three make the court free-blocked, and ready is what makes it free-ready.

9. Needs action — every row the panel can draw

The panel above the board is the desk's only "do this now" surface, and it draws five kinds of row. They share one shape — a tone, a glyph, a title, one line of detail and the single button that clears it — and differ in what put them there and in how late they are. Which rows exist, how late each one is and the order they are drawn in all come from deskActions in src/lib/tournament/desk-actions.ts, read from the board's own court states, so the panel cannot disagree with the tiles above it. Everything here is scoped to the selected day.

Every string below is copied out of desk.tsx and desk-actions.ts — the titles, the details, the button labels, the fold and the empty state. The names, courts and minutes are illustrative instances of that copy, not values from a fixture; 9.1 is the panel as it really stacks, and the cards in 9.2 show one kind at a time only so each condition can be read on its own.

9.1 One panel, in the order it really stacks

Five rows, then a fold. MAX_ACTION_ROWS is five: the panel draws the five most urgent and hides the rest behind Show 2 more, which becomes Show fewer and carries aria-expanded. With five or fewer rows there is no button at all. The fold is screen state, not a preference — it is not persisted, so leaving the Desk forgets it. The list, the fold and the empty state sit in one aria-live="polite" region, so a row appearing is announced rather than waited for.
Rows are re-ranked every thirty seconds. The panel is derived from now, which advances on the same tick as the clock in its header. A ready row becomes a late one the moment its scheduled start passes, and an overrun's rank jumps when the match behind it turns late. Nothing is pinned in place, because the ranking is the panel's whole claim: the top row really is the most overdue thing on the floor. A consequence worth knowing: a row can move, and a folded row can become a shown one, while the host is reading.

9.2 Each kind of row on its own

Two tones remain, after the one that spoke for the organiser's plan was withdrawn. ▶ is the action the desk wants taken now; ! is something allowed that somebody should look at. Amber, never red — a delayed match is not a danger, and nothing here is an error. Every row but the pool's leads with the court, because that is the thing a moderator walks to.

There is no row for a ready match with no court. That pairing appears on the idle tile — "No match scheduled. Ready to place: E. Vo vs F. Do", plus ", N more waiting" — and nowhere in this panel, because every way a match ends up unplaced is either transient or the organiser's call. A result just recorded can unlock a match that the schedule is about to slot; a match with no slot before the close, or a tournament with no courts, or a first build that has not been run, is a planning decision the Schedule page names with its own cause. The one thing a moderator can do about it — put the pairing on a court that is free right now — is that tile. A row here would either duplicate the tile or hand the desk a link to a page where it cannot make the decision.

9.3 How a row earns its place, and its rank

KindRanked byDrawn when
results-late Minutes past the pool's scheduled_end The pool has an end, that end is more than fifteen minutes ago, and the pool is still incomplete. See 9.4.
overrun The larger of minutes past its slot and how late the match behind it is The court's state is overrun and the match is at least max(5, ⅓ of its slot) minutes past it, or the match queued behind it has already passed its start.
start-late Minutes past the queued match's scheduled start The court is free, its next match can start, and that start has passed by at least a minute.
blocked-late The same The same, except readiness is resting, player-busy or teams-missing. The row names the person, because that is what has to be found.
ready Nothing — these sort after every late row, soonest scheduled first The court is free-ready, the next match is not late, and the court has stood free for READY_ALERT_AFTER_MINUTES.
ConstantValueWhat it gates
overrunAlertAfterMinutes(slot) max(5, ⌈slot ÷ 3⌉) How far past its slot a running match must be before the desk asks about it, with no late successor forcing the row earlier. A third because a third game adds about a third of a best-of-three; a fixed number would be half of a short slot and routine for a long one. It is independent of the tile, which turns amber at the slot itself. An assumption until a real tournament has been replayed against it.
READY_ALERT_AFTER_MINUTES 3 How long a court with a ready match must have stood free before the desk asks for it to start. Measured from the latest ended_at on that court; a court with no finished match counts as free since the day began.

9.4 The overdue-pool row, in full

This is the row whose title is longest and whose subject is least obvious, so it gets the detail. MD — Pool B, results incomplete is two identities and one state: the event, then the pool, then what is wrong with it. Nothing in it names a match, because a pool is not a match.

PartWhere it comes from
MD categories.code, read through catMap. The setup step ships five codes and never asks for one: MS Men's Singles, WS Women's Singles, MD Men's Doubles, WD Women's Doubles, XD Mixed Doubles. A code is an identity, not an abbreviation to be re-derived from the name — which is why a renamed event keeps it.
— An em dash joins the event to the pool, the same shape every panel heading uses (MS — Draw A). When the category row has not loaded, the dash goes with the code rather than standing alone, so the title reads Pool B, results incomplete and never undefined — Pool B.
Pool B pools.name, written by generatePoolsForCategory as Pool plus a letter from the alphabet in generation order. It is stored, not computed, so a renamed pool keeps its new name on this row.
, results incomplete The row's own state clause. It is the only part of the title that says anything is wrong; the dash and the two names are identification.
22 min past scheduled finish The detail line, and the row's rank: whole minutes between scheduled_end and now, at least fifteen by the gate. It is recomputed on every tick, so it counts up while the row is on screen, and a schedule rebuild that moves scheduled_end moves the number with it. It is no longer a fixed sentence, because the panel now ranks by lateness: a row that hid its own rank could not be read as first of five.

Four conditions must hold together. Any one of them failing keeps the row off the panel:

ConditionWhat it reads
The pool has a scheduled end pool.scheduled_end is not null. The schedule writes it as max(scheduled_time + slot) over the pool's non-void matches that have a time, so it is the projected end of the pool's last match — a plan figure, not a result. A pool with no time has no end, and therefore no row: the desk cannot call a result late against a finish that was never planned.
It belongs to the selected day dayIdForTime(orderedDays, scheduled_end) equals the day the desk is showing — the same attribution matches use, so a pool that ends after midnight stays on the day it started rather than jumping to the next calendar date. Switching days can empty the row without anything having changed, which is why the panel is read as a day, not as the tournament.
It is not complete poolIsComplete, the same function the pool table reads. A pool of fewer than four teams is incomplete by definition. Above that, every pairing must appear exactly once with a reported winner from one of its two sides; a duplicate pairing or a winner outside the match does not count. So the row clears itself the moment the last missing result is entered — no dismissal exists, and none is needed.
It is past the grace now is later than scheduled_end + 15 minutes. A pool that finished at its planned time but whose results were entered two minutes later never raises the row, and a pool whose last match is still playing has not reached its end at all.
What this row is not. It is not the overrun row: an overrun is one running match past its slot, and its action is to enter that match's result; this is a set of matches whose results are missing, and its action opens the score page with no match chosen. It is not per match — one pool, one row, however many pairings are outstanding. It is not gated by tournament format, deliberately: gating it on "this tournament has pools" removed the row from the format the app actually runs, which states.spec.ts caught by planting a pool inside a placement-only tournament. And it is amber, never red: a missing result is an omission, not a danger.
One consequence worth knowing. A void pairing is not a reported one, so a pool whose last outstanding match was voided rather than played keeps this row — the completion rule counts decided pairings, and a void match decides nothing. That is the pool table's rule as well, deliberately: a desk that cleared the row while the table still called the pool incomplete would be two answers to one question. The row disappears when the remaining pairing is actually played, or when the schedule no longer gives the pool an end at all.

9.7 What the panel decides, and what it refuses to

RuleBehaviour
Who sees itEditors only. A reader gets the board and the progress — the things that answer "what is on each court" without asking them to chase anything they cannot act on. Hidden rather than disabled, and hidden while the access read is still in flight, so nothing that can start a match is drawn for someone who may not.
ScopeThe selected day. A pool belongs to the day its scheduled end falls on, by the same rule as a match, so switching days can empty the panel without anything having changed.
One derivationRows read the board's own DeskCourtStates and the same slot and readiness rules, so a row cannot disagree with the tile it is about. There is no second notion of "late" in the panel.
One match, one rowA match appears at most once: a running match as overrun, a queued match as start-late, blocked-late or ready, never two of those. A court can hold two rows — the match on it and the one behind it — and those are two different matches.
Ranked, and re-rankedLate rows first, most late first, by the figure their own title prints; then the rows that are not late, soonest scheduled first. The order is recomputed every tick, so a row can move when something crosses a threshold.
Five, then foldFive rows are drawn; the rest are behind Show N more. The fold is a count, not a severity filter: what is hidden was ranked last, so nothing urgent is ever only behind the button.
No dismissalThere is no "done" or "snooze". A row leaves when the fact behind it changes: the match starts, the result is recorded, the pool completes, the next match stops being late. Acknowledging a conflict is a different surface (the schedule review), not this one.
A number that explains the orderEvery late title carries the number that ranked it — minutes late, minutes on court, minutes past the finish. It is the same figure the sort used, so the order is readable from the rows themselves instead of being an unexplained arrangement. The one kind with no number is ready, and it sorts last.

10. Invariants the sweep pins

The ruleWhy it exists
The pill must be a discrete surface on its own tile: the chip's fill differs from the tile's by at least 8 RGB units — the tightest separation the shipped treatments already produce (free-ready at 8.1). playing once shipped with its tile painting the pill's own fill — 1.000:1, an uppercase word in green on green with no edge at all — and nothing caught it, because the only scan that had ever run hit an empty tournament with no toned tile.
Colour is never the only channel. Every pill carries a word as well as a fill, and each of the five states has its own label — so the board is readable with no hue at all.
All five states at once only ever happens in a fixture. A live tournament cannot reach it — a court is playing or it is not. Showing the whole wall needs a fixture that plants the results, the clock and the queue.
Every chip clears 4.5:1 between ink and fill, and every state stays distinct in grayscale and under deuteranopia. Measured by flattening the translucent fills the way a browser paints them — chip over tile over page — not by comparing raw computed values. No minimum margin is asserted; a threshold would be a number picked to pass.
free-blocked and idle share the resting hairline; every other state has its own edge. Neither asks for anything. Giving the blocked state its own edge would put two amber surfaces on one tile — the pill already carries that signal.
